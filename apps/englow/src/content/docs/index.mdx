The English alphabet comprises **26 letters** derived from the Latin script, consisting of **5 pure vowels** (A, E, I, O, U), **21 consonants**, and the semi-vowel **Y**. In British English (Received Pronunciation / Modern RP), these 26 letters generate over **44 distinct phonemes** (individual sounds).

Mastering the letter names, International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA) values, and standard British articulation is the foundational cornerstone of our learning portal.

## 🇬🇧 British English Distinctions

While written English shares standard orthography globally, British English retains key phonetic and cultural characteristics:

### 1. The Letter "Z": /zed/ vs /ziː/
In British English and across the Commonwealth, the letter **Z** is strictly pronounced **/zed/** (rhyming with *bed*), never the Americanized */ziː/*.

> [!TIP]
> **Strict Teacher's Pronunciation Rule:**
> Always say **/zed/** for the letter Z. Saying */ziː/* in our classroom will earn you immediate corrective drills!

### 2. British Non-Rhoticity (The Silent 'R')
In standard British Received Pronunciation, the letter **R** is only pronounced if followed immediately by a vowel sound (e.g., *red*, *grow*). When followed by a consonant or appearing at the end of a word, it is not rolled or curled; instead, it lengthens the preceding vowel:
- **Car** → `/kɑː/` (not */kɑːr/*)
- **Water** → `/ˈwɔː.tə/`
- **Park** → `/pɑːk/`

### 3. Pure Vowels vs Diphthongs
British English is renowned for its rich inventory of diphthongs (gliding vowels), particularly the central diphthong **/əʊ/** as in *go*, *home*, and *ocean*.
